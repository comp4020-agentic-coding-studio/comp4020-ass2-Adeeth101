## This week's question

Your garden produces a glut, then produces very little. The household eats at a
constant rate all year.

**How much of the peak must you move into the trough, what does that take in
space and energy, and when does preserving cost more than it saves?**

Note what this week is not. Fermentation creates no calories. It moves them
through time, and loses some in transit.

---

## Harvest against consumption

From week 2 you have a monthly yield curve. Draw consumption flat across it.

- **Surplus** — the area above the line
- **Deficit** — the area below it

**Storage is sized on the smaller of the two.** Preserving more than the trough
can absorb is a slower way of composting it.

Three losses to carry, each stated as your own assumption: trimming and
preparation, fermentation mass loss, and the fraction that fails or is not eaten.

---

## Safety: one recipe, its own conditions

The National Center for Home Food Preservation sauerkraut method, from USDA
Agriculture Information Bulletin 539:

- 25 lb cabbage to ¾ cup canning salt — about **2.25 % salt by weight**
- Ferment at **21–24 °C**, "fully fermented in about 3 to 4 weeks"

This is the only place in the course where a number could hurt somebody, so it is
the one place the course refuses to generalise.

---

## Four things that method does not say

1. It states **no pH figure at all**. The 4.6 threshold is US food law, for sealed shelf-stable product
2. The numbers **do not transfer** to other vegetables
3. **Do not reduce the salt.** The source is explicit
4. It is US extension guidance; there is no Australian equivalent

**You design capacity. You make and eat nothing for this course.**

## Worked example: Adelaide

Annual mean 17.0 °C, no frost days, a long dry autumn. The fermentation space
needs no heating and no cooling in any month, so its **counted energy load is
zero**, and the bench sits inside 21–24 °C for most of the useful season.

Adelaide's harvest curve is also the most concentrated — winter rain against
summer demand. More surplus arriving at once, and a longer trough to cross.

Darwin and Alice Springs will not get zero, and the control load they write down
here is one of the few things that can push a design past 5 kWh/day.

## Then: assemble Assignment 1

**Decisions first, measurements second.**

- Write the *decisions*: connect the house roof or not; keep fish or not; rear indoors or out; plant G3 or leave it lawn
- Then attach measurement rows: what, which decision, method, location, units, frequency, accuracy, cost
- **Cross-check both ways.** Every row points at a decision. Every decision has a row
- Total against the **$1,500** investigation allowance, and record what you dropped
