## This week's question

You know what the garden wants (week 2) and what the household wants (week 1).

**How much water does the site actually produce, and in which months?**

Not the annual total. The months. Annual totals are how people end up with a tank
that runs dry in September.

## enHealth Appendix B

    runoff (L) = A × (rainfall − B) × roof area

- **A** — collection efficiency, 0.80–0.85 in the source, citing Martin (1980). **This course uses 0.80**
- **B** — initial loss, **2 mm per month**, or 24 mm a year

The source's own worked example:

    0.8 × (750 − 24) × 200 = 116,160 L

**If your spreadsheet does not reproduce that line exactly, fix it before going
further.**

Apply it **per month**. First flush: about **10 L per 50 m² of roof** per event.
And the identity behind all of it: 1 mm of rain on 1 m² of roof is 1 L.

---

## Worked example: Alice Springs, the hardest site

350 m² of roof across house and machinery shed. 284 mm of rain a year.

    0.80 × (284 − 24) mm × 350 m² = 72,800 L = 73 kL/year

Domestic demand is 219 kL. **The roof covers a third of the household, and none
of the garden.**

Alice Springs is the only site with a second source: a bore capped at **100 kL a
year**. That gives 173 kL against a domestic demand of 219 kL and a garden asking
660 kL — total demand approaching 880 kL.

---

## What a capped allocation does

It converts a resource question into an **allocation** question.

You are no longer asking how much water exists. You are choosing who gets it.

That is why the excellent Alice Springs reference design reaches **97 % water
closure with 16 % garden water satisfaction** — it closes the loop by watering
almost nothing.

**Both numbers are published together. Always.**
