## This week's question

You have between 130 and 500 m² of growable ground.

The naive sizing — area × days × yield — says the warm site with the long season
wins.

**It does not.** Where does the water come from?

## Three equations, and one habit

**Reference evapotranspiration**, FAO-56 Equation 52 (Hargreaves):

    ETo = 0.0023 × (Tmean + 17.8) × √(Tmax − Tmin) × Ra    [mm/month]

**Crop demand:** ETc = ETo × Kc. Kc = 0.95 for a mixed garden — a **course
assumption**, inside FAO-56 Table 12's range.

**Effective rain**, FAO Irrigation Training Manual 3:

    Pe = 0.8P − 25   for P > 75 mm/month
    Pe = 0.6P − 10   for P ≤ 75 mm/month

Deficit = ETc − Pe, **floored at zero**.

---

## Worked example: Canberra

Annual ETo 1,244 mm against 616 mm of rain. P/ETo = 0.50.

Sum the monthly deficits across the growing season:

    932 mm of net irrigation per m² per year
    932 mm × 450 m² = 419,400 L = 419 kL/year

The roof delivers 142 kL, and the household has first call on it.

**Canberra's garden is water-limited long before it is land-limited.**

---

## What the five sites show

Net irrigation demand per m² is a climate property, not a design choice. It is
highest where ETo is high and rain is low — and the sites do not rank the way
intuition suggests.

Two Canberra advantages no indicator scores: **rainfall evenness** (28 % of rain
in the driest four months, against Darwin's 1.4 %) and **chill**, which the course
handles as a regional category rather than a formula, because the sine-curve
method gives Canberra 1,979 hours against a reported ~720.
