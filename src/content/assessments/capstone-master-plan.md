## The brief

> A year of measurement at your client's home is finished and the findings are in
> front of you. Compare the home as it stands with three feasible retrofit
> options, recommend one, and make the case for it in terms the household can act
> on.

This is the document that goes to the programme. Not a brochure and not a
construction drawing: a comparison with its arithmetic attached, a recommendation
with its uncertainty declared, and an honest account of what you chose not to do.

The judgement being tested is **whether the option you recommend is the one your
own numbers support**. Three options that differ only in scale are not three
options. A recommendation that ignores the household's stated priorities is not a
recommendation.

## Where the numbers come from

Your home's **specialist findings** — the measured year, the soil zones, the
water quality, the flow events, the food-waste audit — are published on your
client's findings page and downloadable as CSV. They are the same for every
student who took that home, **whatever Assignment 1 said**, so nothing here
depends on how that went.

The findings are **synthetic**: generated to be consistent with the home, labelled
that way in every file's first line and on every page. They stand in for a
measured year. They are not one, and a single year — even a real one — does not
establish drought reliability, which is why the separate
[synthetic dry year](/method/datasets/) exists.

## What you submit

1. **The baseline.** The home as it stands, from the findings: what it uses, what
   it imports, what it wastes, what it already has. Every later comparison is
   against this.
2. **Three feasible retrofit options**, each a coherent package rather than a
   shopping list, and each one actually buildable on your site. One of them
   should be noticeably cheaper than the others; that is usually the interesting
   one.
3. **A flow diagram** for the recommended option: water, food and waste streams,
   every arrow labelled with a direction and a quantity, imports drawn crossing
   the boundary rather than omitted.
4. **A conceptual site layout** on your home's plan: where the storage, the
   treatment, the growing area and the sanitation system go, with separation
   distances and access shown. Conceptual, to scale, not a construction drawing.
5. **The important capacities and flows** for each option — storage volume,
   connected catchment, treated volume, irrigated area, peak flow and static
   lift, whichever your options actually turn on. Show the calculation for the
   ones that decide between options.
6. **Cost**, from the [published price schedule](/method/costing/), including the
   10 % contingency. Hard constraint: **$30,000** of new spend. Existing site
   inventory is free.
7. **Energy**, as a peak-month daily average with the month named, counted loads
   with power and duty cycle, uncertain loads at their **upper** estimate, and
   omitted loads named with a range. Hard constraint: **5 kWh/day**.
8. **Reliability and maintenance.** What fails, how often, what it takes to keep
   running, and who does it. Run the recommended option against the dry-year
   dataset and say what gives way first and in which month.
9. **Uncertainty.** What you did not model, what you were unsure of, and a range
   for each. This is marked, and a short list is not a strong one.
10. **The rejected options**, with reasons. Including any subsystem you decided
    against entirely.

Indicative length: 4,000 to 6,000 words excluding tables, drawings and
spreadsheets.

## You may reject subsystems

Nothing in this course requires your final design to include mushrooms, insects,
aquaponics, preservation or a composting toilet. Each was taught so you could
size it and judge it. **Declining one with a reason is a result**, and a common
one: the arithmetic in weeks 3 to 5 exists precisely so that "we sized it and it
does not earn its place on this site" is an answer you can defend.

What is not acceptable is omitting a subsystem silently, or rejecting it with a
preference rather than a number.

## What this proposal is not

- **Not a loan approval.** The programme is fiction and there is no credit
  assessment anywhere in this course — no interest rates, no income tests, no
  financial advice.
- **Not a construction-certified design.** No pipe diameters, no wiring diagrams,
  no structural detail, no certification. You specify performance and how a
  builder would demonstrate it has been met; licensed trades do the rest later.
- **Not a demonstration of every technology taught.** See above.

## Hard constraints

These are checked before the criteria are applied.

| Constraint | Limit | How it is judged |
|---|---|---|
| Process energy | 5 kWh/day, peak month, counted loads | Uncertain loads at their **upper** estimate. A load you omitted without naming it is a failure, not a saving |
| Budget | $30,000 of new spend | Priced from the published schedule, including the 10 % contingency. Existing site inventory is free |
| Safety and compliance | The course design rules | Each stated with its jurisdiction, and distinguished from what the law requires |

A design that fits the budget by $1,000 is not comfortably compliant. The
reference designs themselves clear it by $1,125 at Darwin, which is inside the
noise of a screening model priced from retail listings, and the course says so
rather than pretending otherwise.

## How performance bands are used

Your reported indicators are placed against your own site's
[reference bands](/method/). **Bands are evidence inside the criteria, never an
automatic grade**, and **water closure is never reported without garden water
satisfaction beside it** — a high closure with a dry garden reads exactly as what
it is.

Bands do not compare you to other students, and they do not guarantee equal
outcomes across sites; the stability testing behind them is
[published with its limits](/method/#how-stable-are-the-bands).
