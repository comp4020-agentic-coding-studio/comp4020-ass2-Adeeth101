## The question

Stand at the fence line of a suburban block for a year and count what crosses
it. Water in. Food in. Fertiliser in. Rubbish out, sewage out, stormwater out.
**Which of those flows could be turned around to feed another system on the same
block, and how much of each would that actually absorb?**

That is the whole course. Everything after this week is one flow, sized properly.

## The household, fixed for everyone

Every student designs for the same four people — two adults and two children aged
9 to 13 — so that five different climates are the only thing that differs.

| Demand | Figure | Where it comes from |
|---|---|---|
| Food energy | 40.0 MJ/day, or 3.49 million kcal/year | NHMRC estimated energy requirements: 13.3 + 10.8 MJ for the adults at PAL 1.8, 8.3 + 7.6 for the children at PAL 1.6 |
| Protein | 185 g/day, or 67.5 kg/year | NHMRC recommended dietary intakes: 64 + 46 + 40 + 35 g |
| Domestic water | 150 L/person/day, or 219 kL/year | **Course assumption**, at the midpoint of enHealth's sourced 100–200 L/person/day indoor range |
| Excreted nitrogen | 13.6 kg/year | Jönsson et al. 2004: 4.55 kg N per adult-equivalent, children counted as half an adult (that halving is a course assumption) |
| Household food waste | 380 kg/year | National Food Waste Strategy feasibility study, about 95 kg per person |

Read that table twice. It is the denominator of every score you will get.

## The method: a closure ratio that cannot be gamed

A self-sufficiency ratio that divides *production* by demand has two well-known
faults. It can exceed 100 % — so a design is rewarded for growing food nobody
eats — and it ignores imports entirely, so a system running on bought-in fish
feed scores the same as one running on its own.

Closure is defined to remove both:

> **Closure = what the site supplied and the household actually used ÷ everything
> the household used, including imports. Capped at 100 %.**

Three consequences worth stating now, because they will each surprise someone in
about week 5:

1. **Imports sit in the denominator.** Carting water in lowers water closure; it
   does not simply vanish from the sum.
2. **Animal products count only in proportion to feed grown on site.** Fish fed
   on bought pellets are bought fish.
3. **A bigger garden cannot lower your water score.** An earlier version of this
   model put the student's chosen garden in the denominator, and a *more*
   ambitious design scored *worse*. That was a bug in the metric, not a finding
   about gardens.

## Worked example: the roof, at every site

The one calculation everybody can do in week 1. Roof runoff, from enHealth's
Appendix B, citing Martin (1980):

> runoff (L) = A × (rainfall − B) × roof area, with A = 0.80 and B = 2 mm per month

Canberra, 300 m² of roof, 616 mm of rain a year:

    0.80 × (616 − 24) mm × 300 m² = 142,080 L = 142 kL

Domestic demand is 219 kL. So the largest roof in the set, in a climate nobody
calls dry, delivers **65 % of the household's water before a single plant is
watered**. Alice Springs delivers 73 kL, a third of demand. Darwin delivers 411
kL, nearly twice demand — and still runs out in September, for reasons week 9 is
entirely about.

## What is out of scope

Energy and shelter. Not because they do not matter, but because crediting them
here would let a design claim self-sufficiency it has not demonstrated. Biogas is
a case in point: your digester will make it, you will record it, and **it earns
you nothing in this course**. Only the digestate's nutrients count. Those belong
to **SLOP4762 — Designing the Closed-Loop Household II: Energy and Shelter**, a
proposed follow-on course that adds energy physics and introductory
thermodynamics to the prerequisites.

## What this week hands you

The **site sheet**: your preset's plot, growing area, roof, existing tanks and
bore allocation, with twelve rows of Bureau of Meteorology monthly climate behind
it and a source for every value. Every later week writes into it. Bring it to
every tutorial.
