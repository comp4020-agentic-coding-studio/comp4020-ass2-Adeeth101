## The question

Your garden does not produce evenly. It produces a glut, and then it produces
very little. The household eats at a constant rate all year.

**How much of the peak must you move into the trough, what does that take in
space and energy, and at what point does preserving cost more than it saves?**

Note what this week is not. Fermentation creates no calories. It moves them
through time, and it loses some in transit.

## The method: harvest curve against consumption curve

From week 2 you have a monthly yield curve. Draw the household's consumption as a
flat line across it. The area where harvest exceeds consumption is your surplus;
the area where it falls short is your deficit. **Storage capacity is sized on the
smaller of those two areas**, because preserving more than the trough can absorb
is just a slower way of composting it.

Three losses to carry: trimming and preparation waste, fermentation mass loss, and
the fraction that fails or is not eaten. This course asks you to state each as an
assumption rather than supplying a number, because they depend on what you grow.

## Safety: one recipe, its own conditions, and nothing beyond that

This is the only place in the course where a number could hurt somebody, so it is
the one place the course refuses to generalise.

The **National Center for Home Food Preservation** sauerkraut method, adapted from
USDA Agriculture Information Bulletin 539:

- 25 lb of cabbage to ¾ cup of canning salt — about **2.25 % salt by weight**
- Ferment at **70–75 °F (21–24 °C)**, "fully fermented in about 3 to 4 weeks"
- At 60–65 °F it takes 5 to 6 weeks; below 60 °F it may not ferment; above 75 °F
  it may go soft

Four things that method does **not** say, all of which are routinely attributed to
it:

1. **It states no pH figure at all.** The pH 4.6 threshold comes from US food law,
   where it is the boundary below which *Clostridium botulinum* spores will not
   germinate **in a sealed, shelf-stable product**. It is not a general pathogen
   boundary: *E. coli* O157:H7, *Salmonella* and *Listeria* are unaffected by it.
2. **The numbers do not transfer to other vegetables.** Salt percentage, the
   vegetable's own sugars and its buffering capacity were validated together, for
   cabbage. A different vegetable is a different system.
3. **Do not reduce the salt.** The source is explicit: "Do not attempt to make
   sauerkraut or fermented pickles by cutting back on the salt required."
4. **It is US extension guidance.** There is no equivalent Australian
   home-fermentation standard.

A separate university extension source gives 2–2.5 % salt but a different
schedule, 68–72 °F for 7–14 days. Both are tested; **mixing them is not**. An
earlier draft of this course did exactly that, which is why you are reading this
paragraph.

You will design preservation capacity. You will not make or eat anything for this
course.

## Worked example: Adelaide

Adelaide is the mildest site in the set — annual mean 17.0 °C, no frost days, and
a long dry autumn. Its fermentation space needs no heating and no cooling in any
month, so its counted energy load for this subsystem is **zero**, and its bench
sits at ambient inside the stated 21–24 °C band for most of the useful season.

Its harvest curve is also the most concentrated: winter rain against summer
demand gives Adelaide the lowest water closure of the five sites, which pushes its
production into the months when rain and warmth briefly coincide. More surplus
arriving at once, and a longer trough to cross.

That combination — a sharp glut and a free bench — is why Adelaide leads this
week, having led week 5 as the site where seasonal species switching hurts.

## Bokashi and silage: the arrow into week 10

The same microbiology does a second job. Bokashi pre-treatment and silage both
acidify organic material before it reaches the compost bay or the digester,
lowering odour and stabilising nitrogen that would otherwise volatilise.

That arrow belongs in your week 10 mass balance. Draw it now.

## Five sites

| Site | Ambient control needed | What the site does to the curve |
|---|---|---|
| Adelaide | None | Sharp glut, long trough. The best fermentation site and the one with most to preserve |
| Canberra | None most of the year; winter is below band | Frost ends the season cleanly, so the glut is predictable and the trough is deep |
| Brisbane | Occasional summer cooling | The flattest harvest curve of the set: least to preserve, because least goes to waste |
| Alice Springs | **Summer cooling, counted** | Above-band for months; an uncontrolled bench will overshoot 75 °F badly |
| Darwin | **Cooling most of the year, counted** | Annual mean 27.7 °C. Ambient fermentation is out of band nearly always, and the load is real |
