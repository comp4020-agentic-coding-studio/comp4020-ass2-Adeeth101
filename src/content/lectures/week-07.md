## The question

You now know what the garden wants (week 2) and what the household wants
(week 1). **How much water does the site actually produce, and in which months
does it produce it?**

Not the annual total. The months. Annual totals are how people end up with a tank
that runs dry in September.

## The method: enHealth Appendix B

    runoff (L) = A × (rainfall − B) × roof area

- **A** is collection efficiency, 0.80–0.85 in the source, citing Martin (1980).
  This course uses **0.80**.
- **B** is initial loss, **2 mm per month**, or 24 mm a year.

The source's own worked example: 0.8 × (750 − 24) × 200 = 116,160 L. If your
spreadsheet does not reproduce that line exactly, fix it before going further.

Apply it **per month**, not to the annual total, or a month with 1 mm of rain
contributes a negative number.

Two supporting rules. First flush: divert about **10 L per 50 m² of roof** at the
start of each event — a figure confirmed verbatim on the Australian Government's
Your Home rainwater page. And the useful identity behind all of it: 1 mm of rain
on 1 m² of roof is 1 L.

## Worked example: Alice Springs, the hardest site there is

Alice Springs has the largest roof in the set after Canberra — 350 m² across house
and machinery shed — and the least rain: 284 mm a year.

    0.80 × (284 − 24) mm × 350 m² = 72,800 L = 73 kL/year

Domestic demand is 219 kL. **The roof covers a third of the household, and none of
the garden.**

Alice Springs is the only site with a second source: a bore allocation capped at
**100 kL/year**. Add it and the site has 173 kL against a domestic demand of 219 kL
and a garden asking for 1,650 mm per m² — which over 400 m² is 660 kL. Total
demand approaching 880 kL against a supply of 173 kL.

This is why the excellent Alice Springs reference design reaches **97 % water
closure with 16 % garden water satisfaction**. It closes the loop by watering
almost nothing. Both numbers are published together, always, precisely so that a
design cannot claim the first without disclosing the second.

Notice what a capped allocation does to the design problem: it converts a resource
question into an allocation question. You are no longer asking how much water
exists. You are choosing who gets it.

| Site | Roof | Rain | Roof yield | As a share of domestic demand | Other source |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Darwin | 300 m² | 1,733 mm | **411 kL** | 188 % | — |
| Brisbane | 170 m² | 1,146 mm | 153 kL | 70 % | — |
| Canberra | 300 m² | 616 mm | 142 kL | 65 % | — |
| Adelaide | 230 m² | 525 mm | 92 kL | 42 % | — |
| Alice Springs | 350 m² | 284 mm | **73 kL** | 33 % | Bore, 100 kL/yr capped |

Darwin collects nearly twice what the household drinks and still imports water in
the dry season. Holding both of those facts at once is what week 9 is for.
