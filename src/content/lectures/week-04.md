## The question

Search for a closed-loop household diagram and you will find this arrow: kitchen
scraps feed black soldier fly larvae, larvae feed the fish, fish feed you. It is
drawn as a closed circle roughly every time.

**Size it. What fraction of the fish's feed do your own scraps actually cover?**

## The method: thresholds and dry-matter bioconversion

Black soldier fly larvae have a **lower developmental threshold of 10.4–12.3 °C**;
below it, development stops. Egg to adult takes 91 days at 19 °C, 42 at 28 °C and
32 at 31 °C, with best population growth near 30 °C. This course screens each
site with a linear productive factor ramping from 12 °C to 27 °C — a
simplification, and labelled one.

Conversion is measured on **dry matter**: on food waste, larvae achieve about
52 % substrate reduction and **28 % bioconversion** over 14 days. This course uses
20 % as a conservative course assumption.

## Worked example: Darwin, the best site there is

Darwin's productive factor is 0.97 — larvae are thermally productive essentially
all year. Start from the household's 380 kg of food waste:

| Step | Factor | Running total |
|---|---|---|
| Household food waste | — | 380 kg/yr |
| Plant-based share (course rule, below) | × 0.60 | 228 kg/yr |
| Dry matter | × 0.25 | 57 kg DM/yr |
| Bioconversion to larvae | × 0.20 | 11.4 kg larvae DM/yr |
| Darwin thermal factor | × 0.97 | **11.1 kg larvae DM/yr** |

Now the demand side. A 4 m² aquaponic grow bed at the FAO feed-rate ratio of
50 g/m²/day, running twelve months:

    50 g/m² × 4 m² × 365 d = 73 kg of feed per year

Allowing larvae to make up at most 90 % of the ration:

    11.1 ÷ (73 × 0.9) = 17 %

**Seventeen per cent, at the best site in the set, with the whole household's
plant-based scraps committed.** An 8 m² bed halves it to 8 %. Canberra, with a
thermal factor of 0.19, would manage 3 % outdoors.

The other 83 % is bought feed. Under this course's closure definition, fish grown
on bought feed are bought fish, so a Darwin excellent design counts **0.27 kg** of
fish protein a year against a household requirement of 67.5 kg.

That is not a flaw in your design. It is the finding.

### The fairness lever

Rearing indoors at about 20 °C gives a productive factor of roughly 0.53
*anywhere*. It converts a climate advantage into an energy cost, which is exactly
the trade this course wants you to make explicitly rather than accidentally.

## Substrate: a course rule, and what the law actually says

**The course rule.** Insect substrates are plant-based. Larvae destined for fish
are heat-treated before feeding. Meat, dairy and fish sludge go to compost or the
digester, never to larvae.

**What the law actually says.** Australian restricted-animal-material rules
prohibit feeding vertebrate-derived material to **ruminants**, and mammal products
to **pigs**. Swill feeding is prohibited nationally and expressly covers pet and
hobby animals. None of it mentions fish, aquaculture or insects. A household
feeding its own fish is not captured.

The claim that insect substrates must exclude meat and catering waste appears in
one academic review and is published by no Australian regulator.

So the course is deliberately stricter than the law, for a biosecurity reason
worth understanding: the pathway a rule closes matters more than whether the rule
happens to bind you. **Learning to tell a design rule from a legal requirement is
the transferable skill here**, and week 11 tests it again on greywater.

## Five sites

| Site | Thermal factor | Outdoor rearing | Indoor at 20 °C |
|---|---|---|---|
| Darwin | 0.97 | All year | No reason to |
| Alice Springs | 0.58 | Strong spring to autumn; winter nights stop it | Worth it for continuity |
| Brisbane | 0.57 | Most of the year; space is the constraint, not heat | Likely, for odour and neighbour management |
| Adelaide | 0.33 | Four to five good months | Yes, and cheaply |
| Canberra | **0.19** | Barely viable outdoors | Effectively required, at roughly 0.5 kWh/day of counted load |
